\title{CLAE: A Compact 12.5~Hz Bengali Speech Representation Encoder}
\author{Anonymous authors}

\begin{document}

\begin{abstract}
We present CLAE, a continuous-latent encoder for Bengali speech that produces 256-dimensional features at 12.5 frames/s with 15.29M parameters. Trained on approximately 2,000 hours of speech, CLAE combines waveform reconstruction, non-contrastive agreement between augmented views, and frame-level distribution regularization. Frozen-feature evaluations show better emotion and speaker discrimination than a random encoder, while specialized pretrained models remain stronger on most tasks. In matched ablations, removing reconstruction degrades reconstruction and temporal-emotion performance; adding view agreement and regularization to reconstruction alone yields mixed gains. Doubling the frame rate improves several metrics, exposing a resolution--performance trade-off. Age prediction does not exceed a majority-label reference. These single-seed comparisons support a compact continuous representation for the evaluated tasks, while leaving generalization, training variability, and perceptual reconstruction quality unresolved.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

Compact speech encoders are useful when their frozen features retain information for several downstream discrimination tasks without requiring task-specific encoder adaptation. This frozen-feature framing follows speech representation work such as wav2vec~2.0 and HuBERT \citep{baevski2020wav2vec2,hsu2021hubert} and the lightweight-head evaluation protocol of SUPERB \citep{yang2021superb}. Modern neural audio codecs and tokenizers also commonly operate at low temporal resolutions, making 12.5~Hz a practical design point for a compact speech representation; DualCodec and NanoCodec, for example, report 12.5~Hz codec configurations \citep{li2025dualcodec,casanova2025nanocodec}. This does not make the systems bitrate- or architecture-equivalent. CLAE is continuous rather than a codec: its floating-point latent is neither quantized nor entropy-coded, so its scalar rate is not directly comparable to codec bitrate.

We present CLAE, a continuous-latent speech autoencoder trained on approximately 2,000 hours from four Bengali speech sources. Its 15.29M-parameter frontend and encoder produce a 256-dimensional frame-level representation $z$ at 12.5~Hz; downstream probes keep this feature extractor frozen and consume $z$. A distinct 64-dimensional projector output $p$ is used only by two representation objectives: direct global/local view consistency and Variance-Invariance-Sketching Regularization (VISReg; \citealp{wu2026visreg}). View consistency encourages features stable to the augmentation and masking nuisances used to construct the views. VISReg regularizes the distribution of projector features to discourage collapse. An auxiliary decoder reconstructs the clean source from a corrupted copy of $z$, using an 80-bin log-mel loss to encourage information retention.

Our contribution is an empirical study of this combined design for compact Bengali speech representations. We adapt frame-level view agreement and distribution regularization to a low-rate Conformer-derived encoder, assess its frozen features against pretrained speech models, and use matched ablations to examine the objectives, frame rate, decoder corruption, and residual connections. The architectural and objective components build on prior work; the experiments test their combination rather than claim each component as a new method.

\section{Related Work}

\paragraph{Joint-embedding learning.}
Image JEPA introduced representation-space prediction with distinct context and target pathways \citep{assran2023ijepa}; A-JEPA adapted masked-target JEPA ideas to audio \citep{fei2023ajepa}. LeJEPA instead studies direct view agreement with isotropic-Gaussian regularization \citep{balestriero2025lejepa}. CLAE uses a JEPA-inspired, non-contrastive per-frame view-agreement loss, but it is not canonical predictive JEPA: it has one shared encoder and no target encoder, exponential moving average, learned predictor, stop-gradient target path, masked-region prediction, or negative pairs. It also differs from LeJEPA in domain, waveform view construction, grouped consistency loss, frame-level population, supporting regularizer, and reconstruction.

\paragraph{Distribution regularization.}
VICReg combines invariance, per-dimension variance control, and covariance regularization \citep{bardes2022vicreg}. VISReg retains explicit center and scale terms but replaces covariance control with random one-dimensional projections whose sorted samples are matched to Gaussian quantiles \citep{wu2026visreg}. We use VISReg to regularize the distribution of projector features and discourage collapse. Our frame-level adaptation is described in Section~\ref{sec:visreg}.

\paragraph{Continuous and discrete audio representations.}
Continuous Audio Language Models use a continuous variational bottleneck, KL regularization, additional speech distillation, and generative next-frame modeling \citep{rouard2025calm}; those components are not used here. UniWav studies joint representation and generation objectives for speech \citep{liu2025uniwav}. By contrast, UniAudio and UniTok-Audio illustrate autoregressive generation over discrete audio tokens \citep{yang2024uniaudio,liu2025unitokaudio}. Neural codecs such as SoundStream and EnCodec learn quantized, bitrate-controlled representations \citep{zeghidour2021soundstream,defossez2023encodec}. Mimi, introduced with Moshi, is a separately pretrained common-evaluation reconstruction reference in our ablation study \citep{defossez2024moshi}. CLAE's continuous floating-point latent is neither quantized nor entropy-coded, so its scalar rate is not directly comparable to codec bitrate.

\paragraph{Sequence encoder and residual connections.}
The encoder builds on the Conformer macaron block \citep{gulati2020conformer}. It incorporates squeeze-and-excitation but does not adopt the full downsampling and local-attention design of FastConformer \citep{rekesh2023fastconformer}. Selected layers use an adaptation of Manifold-Constrained Hyper-Connections \citep{xie2025mhc}.

\section{Method}

\subsection{Architecture}

Figure~\ref{fig:architecture} summarizes the architecture. For a 16~kHz mono waveform $x$, the convolutional frontend has five Conv1D stages with channels $[128,256,384,512,512]$, kernels $[10,8,8,4,4]$, and strides $[5,4,4,4,4]$, with GroupNorm and GELU. Its total stride is 1,280 samples, yielding approximately 12.5 frames/s. An eight-layer, 256-dimensional Conformer-derived encoder uses eight attention heads, a 1,024-dimensional feed-forward sublayer, rotary full self-attention, macaron feed-forward modules, a 9-tap convolution branch, dropout 0.1, and squeeze-and-excitation. It produces
\begin{equation}
z = E(F(x)), \qquad z\in\mathbb{R}^{T'\times256}.
\end{equation}
A per-frame MLP projector with dimensions $256\rightarrow512\rightarrow64$ produces $p=P(z)$. The decoder and downstream frozen-feature probes use $z$, whereas the consistency and VISReg objectives use $p$.

The FiLM-conditioned waveform decoder starts with 768 channels, reverses the frontend with strides $[4,4,4,4,5]$, and uses two residual blocks per upsampling stage with dilations $[1,3,9]$. The full training model has 23,803,125 parameters. The frontend, encoder, projector, and decoder contain 2,890,240, 12,403,220, 165,440, and 8,344,225 parameters, respectively. The frozen frontend--encoder feature extractor therefore contains 15,293,460 parameters.

\subsection{Waveform views and consistency objective}

Training first samples one three-second source segment, and every view is derived from that same pre-cropped waveform. Two global views receive independently sampled waveform augmentation. Four local views remain full-length three-second waveforms but receive stronger waveform augmentation and waveform-aligned span masking; their frontend features can receive an additional independent frame mask. The waveform augmentations are gain, additive noise, centered low-pass filtering, and clipping only: they do not shift, speed-perturb, resample, independently crop, or temporally warp the signal. Waveform masks are constructed on the 1,280-sample frontend-frame grid and expanded onto the corresponding waveform samples; the optional frontend mask is also defined on that frame grid. Thus, frame index $t$ refers to the same time interval across views, although its waveform content may be augmented or masked. All six views pass through the same frontend, encoder, and projector.

Let $p_g$ and $p_l$ denote projector features grouped into global and local views. For each example and frame, the center $\bar p_g$ is the mean of the two global projector features. The implemented consistency loss is
\begin{equation}
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{view}}=
\operatorname{mean}\!\left[(p_g-\bar p_g)^2\right]
+\operatorname{mean}\!\left[(p_l-\bar p_g)^2\right].
\label{eq:view}
\end{equation}
Averaging within each group gives global and local views equal aggregate weight despite their different counts. The loss encourages agreement across aligned frames under the specified augmentations. It acts on $p$ without utterance identities or class labels; downstream discrimination is evaluated separately on the frozen encoder features $z$.

\subsection{VISReg adaptation}
\label{sec:visreg}

Figure~\ref{fig:visreg} shows the frame-level regularizer. We flatten projector features into a population of $B=T'VB_{\mathrm{global}}$ points in $D=64$ dimensions, where $V=6$ is the number of views and $B_{\mathrm{global}}$ is the source-segment batch size across workers. Frames are gathered across workers without temporal pooling. For population points $q_i\in\mathbb{R}^D$, it penalizes the population mean, centers the points, computes a per-dimension RMS scale with division by $\sqrt{B}$, and penalizes deviation from unit scale. Normalization uses a detached scale. On every forward pass, 256 normalized Gaussian directions are sampled. Values projected onto each direction are sorted and matched to standard-normal quantiles $\Phi^{-1}(i/(B+1))$. Center, scale, and projected-distribution shape terms have equal internal weight.

\begin{figure*}[t]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{figures/tikz/visreg_adaptation.pdf}
\caption{Frame-level VISReg adaptation. (a) All projector frames form one population. (b) Center and scale penalties constrain its first two moments; normalization uses a detached scale. (c--d) Sorted random projections are matched to standard-normal quantiles. The regularizer sums the center, scale, and shape terms with equal internal weights. Tiles and directions are schematic, not measured activations.}
\label{fig:visreg}
\end{figure*}

This implements the core VISReg center/scale/shape construction with CLAE-specific frame-level and distributed adaptations. Our adaptation gathers frames across workers, adds $10^{-6}$ to the scale for numerical stability, assigns equal weights to the three terms, and casts target quantiles to the activation dtype. Training uses BF16 precision.

\subsection{Reconstruction and total objective}

The decoder receives $z$ from the first global view. Before decoding, an independent span mask removes 50\% of frames in spans of 4--24 frames and Gaussian noise with standard deviation 0.03 is added. The target remains the clean source waveform. The decoder outputs a waveform, which is compared with the clean waveform using an 80-bin log-mel loss with FFT size 1,024, window length 1,024, hop length 256, a Hann window, magnitude weight 1.0, log-magnitude weight 1.0, and spectral-convergence weight 0. Multi-resolution STFT is used only for evaluation.

The training objective is
\begin{equation}
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{total}}=
1.0\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{mel}}+
0.3\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{view}}+
0.7\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{VISReg}}.
\end{equation}
The three coefficients weight losses with different numerical scales. Training uses no quantization, variational bottleneck, or adversarial objective.

\subsection{mHC adaptation}

At the third and sixth encoder layers, mHC widens the residual path to two streams. It applies learned static pre/post mixing and a Sinkhorn-constrained residual matrix with identity blending, uses ten Sinkhorn iterations, wraps complete encoder layers, and returns to one stream by a CLAE-specific uniform mean readout. The resulting residual block is a compact adaptation of mHC. Section~\ref{sec:ablation} reports mixed, probe-dependent evidence for this component.

\section{Data and Training}

The training manifests combine four Bengali speech sources, with 1,310,076 utterances and close to 2,000 total hours of audio: Common Voice \citep{ardila2020commonvoice}, OpenSLR-53 \citep{kjartansson2018crowdsourced}, RegSpeech12 \citep{hassan2025regspeech12}, and Shrutilipi \citep{bhogale2023shrutilipi}. Table~\ref{tab:training-data} gives the source inventory. The manifests use a 95/5 stratified train/validation split with seed 42; Table~\ref{tab:training-split} gives the resulting counts. No exact audited unique duration is available. Common Voice and OpenSLR-53 both contribute to pretraining, so evaluations on those sources are in-domain frozen-feature evaluations with unassessed train--evaluation overlap. The inventory does not establish demographic or dialect balance, and no deduplication or train--evaluation leakage audit was performed. SUBESCO \citep{sultana2021subesco}, used for emotion evaluation, is absent from the documented training inventory.

We report the terminal checkpoint after 210,000 training steps, without downstream-metric checkpoint selection. Training uses BF16, AdamW, batch size 42, gradient accumulation over four batches, and gradient clipping at 1.0. The learning-rate schedule was adjusted during training: the base learning rate was reduced to $5\times10^{-4}$ at step 50,000, and later continuations used $10^{-4}$ with a 300,000-step scheduler horizon. Training ended at step 210,000. Thus, the trajectory includes manual schedule changes rather than a single uninterrupted cosine schedule.

With batch size 42 and four accumulation batches on one worker, the nominal effective batch is 168 source segments. If this batch construction is maintained across the trajectory, 210,000 training steps correspond to 35.28M sampled three-second segments, or 29,400 hours of exposure including repeated sampling. These are exposure estimates, not audited counts of unique audio or successful parameter updates. Each source segment produces six augmented views.

There is no active in-loop validation or validation-based early stopping. The terminal checkpoint is reported without a downstream-metric sweep.

\section{Evaluation}
\label{sec:evaluation}

We assess waveform reconstruction and the information available to lightweight probes trained on frozen encoder features. The full-data protocol evaluates the main pretraining run against external references. A reduced fixed-subset protocol provides the complete six-condition ablation comparison. Their datasets, sample budgets, and probe training budgets differ; scores are compared within each protocol. Appendix~\ref{sec:replication} gives the extraction, split, and scoring details.

Reconstruction is evaluated with MR-STFT, PESQ-WB, STOI, and ESTOI \citep{yamamoto2020parallelwavegan,rix2001pesq,taal2010stoi,jensen2016estoi}. These objective measures capture complementary spectral and intelligibility properties but do not replace listener judgments. External frozen-feature references are WavLM \citep{chen2022wavlm}, Whisper-tiny \citep{radford2023whisper}, ECAPA-TDNN \citep{desplanques2020ecapa}, emotion2vec \citep{ma2024emotion2vec}, Mimi \citep{defossez2024moshi}, and Higgs Audio V2 \citep{boson2025higgsaudiov2tokenizer}. Their training objectives, data, and specializations differ. We include an architecture-matched random CLAE encoder to isolate the benefit of pretraining and a training-fold majority-label predictor for the demographic tasks.

\subsection{Main pretraining run}

Reconstruction uses 400 fixed-order, three-second Common Voice Bengali validation clips with FP32 inference. The reconstruction sample records identify Common Voice 26.0 (June 2026). Generic emotion uses 7,000 SUBESCO utterances with five speaker-disjoint folds. Temporal attention uses 1,708 training and 392 held-out utterances; the temporal Transformer uses four speaker-disjoint folds over 7,000 utterances. Age and gender use five speaker-disjoint Common Voice folds with 15,475 and 15,690 utterances, respectively.

Closed-set speaker identification uses 167 OpenSLR-53 speakers, with 666 training and 334 test utterances. Here the test utterances belong to the same enrolled speakers as the training utterances. Verification instead scores all unique pairs among 2,000 utterances from 334 speakers using cosine similarity. This all-pairs diagnostic is not a standard enrollment/test benchmark. Common Voice and OpenSLR-53 contribute to pretraining, and overlap with these evaluation samples has not been audited.

\paragraph{Contribution of the objectives.}
Representation-only is a negative reconstruction control and trails all other trained CLAE conditions on the reduced representation tasks. This supports retaining reconstruction in the combined design under the evaluated budget, but does not by itself diagnose representation collapse. Conversely, reconstruction-only has the highest generic-emotion F1 among the matched CLAE conditions (43.89\%, versus 41.59\% for the full objective). The full objective is slightly higher on age and both temporal-emotion probes, while reconstruction-only is higher on gender, speaker identification, and verification. These small, mixed differences do not establish a consistent benefit from adding view agreement and VISReg.

\paragraph{Frame rate and architecture.}
The 25~Hz condition improves reconstruction proxies, generic emotion, gender, speaker identification, temporal-Transformer F1, and verification relative to the 12.5~Hz full objective, but lowers age and temporal-attention F1. Its stronger scores come with twice as many latent frames. Removing mHC also produces mixed results: speaker identification, gender, and verification improve, whereas emotion and age F1 decrease. Neither change supports a uniform advantage across tasks.

\paragraph{Decoder corruption and external references.}
Removing decoder corruption gives the strongest PESQ, STOI, ESTOI, age, speaker-identification, and temporal-emotion results among CLAE conditions. The longer-trained main model has the lowest MR-STFT. All CLAE age F1 scores remain below the majority-label reference. Whisper-tiny leads generic emotion among the external models, Mimi leads age, and ECAPA leads gender, speaker identification, and verification. WavLM has the strongest evaluated temporal Transformer. The full-objective CLAE model and Mimi show a metric-dependent reconstruction trade-off on these reduced inputs, rather than the ordering observed under the full-data protocol.

\paragraph{Complementary evidence.}
An earlier evaluation of the matched ablations used 400 reconstruction clips and larger temporal-emotion datasets (Table~\ref{tab:ablation}). It also shows worse performance after removing reconstruction and mixed effects of mHC. That study lacks reconstruction-only results and records a failed no-decoder-corruption reconstruction task; these gaps are not filled with reduced-subset measurements. Table~\ref{tab:appendix-full-suite-a} provides additional full-data results, including three-seed neural-probe summaries and the ASR reference. Figure~\ref{fig:ablation-training-curves} records the matched training trajectories. Training losses describe optimization under each objective; they are not validation results and do not rank downstream generalization.

\section{Limitations}
\label{sec:limitations}

Training did not use validation-based model selection. The main comparisons and matched ablations use one probe seed. Without confidence intervals or independent pretraining replications, small differences cannot establish statistical significance or training stability. No human listening study was conducted, so reconstruction evidence is limited to objective proxy metrics. The saved SI-SDR measurements are unexpectedly low (about $-23$ to $-25$ dB for reconstruction-capable CLAE conditions in the reduced study, versus $+2.05$ dB for Mimi). Their disagreement with the spectral and intelligibility proxies remains unresolved; we withhold SI-SDR from the comparison tables pending a waveform-alignment and phase-fidelity investigation. The remaining metrics do not establish sample-level waveform fidelity. We did not compare alternative distribution regularizers under a controlled protocol.

The training inventory was not audited for demographic or dialect coverage, deduplication, or leakage. Common Voice and OpenSLR evaluations are in-domain because both sources contribute to pretraining, and their train--evaluation overlap is unknown. Demographic probes predict dataset-supplied labels rather than independently verified age or gender. Historical corpus versions are incompletely documented, limiting exact replication and interpretation beyond these samples. ASR is a development-set diagnostic, so we draw no conclusion about linguistic retention. The findings concern the described frame-level VISReg and compact mHC adaptations; alternative regularizers and independent pretraining replications remain untested.

\section{Ethical Considerations}

Speech can identify speakers and reveal sensitive attributes. The age and gender probes measure predictability of corpus labels; they do not validate those labels as a complete account of identity or justify individual-level decisions. Speaker representations can enable unauthorized identification, surveillance, or impersonation. These research evaluations do not establish suitability for deployment in such settings. Bengali speech and acted-emotion results also should not be generalized to unrepresented dialects, populations, or spontaneous emotion.

The study uses third-party speech corpora. Their availability does not by itself establish consent for demographic inference or speaker identification. The corpus-specific consent terms, release versions, and redistribution permissions require verification before any audio redistribution; this paper makes no claim that those permissions have been independently audited. No new human listening study or participant recruitment was conducted.

\paragraph{Availability.}
We plan to release the source code, evaluation recipes, and model checkpoints through a public repository. Dataset manifests will not be released. Third-party audio must be obtained from its providers under the applicable terms. Without released manifests, exact reconstruction of the sampled subsets will be limited; sample counts, label inventories, and split rules are provided for interpreting and replicating the protocols.

\section{Conclusion}

CLAE learns compact continuous Bengali speech features through reconstruction, view agreement, and frame-level distribution regularization. Its frozen encoder improves emotion and speaker discrimination over a random control, although specialized pretrained references remain stronger on most tasks and age prediction does not exceed the majority baseline. Matched ablations support retaining reconstruction, while the added representation objectives, mHC, and decoder corruption have task-dependent effects. The 25~Hz variant exposes a trade-off between temporal resolution and predictive performance. These findings motivate further study of compact continuous encoders with audited data splits, independent training replications, and perceptual evaluation.

\section{Replication Details}
\label{sec:replication}

CLAE probes use frozen final encoder features $z$, without layer aggregation or projector features. Audio is mixed to mono and resampled to 16 kHz. CLAE encodes non-overlapping three-second windows, right-pads the final window, and concatenates their frames. Generic utterance probes pool all returned frames, including final-window padding. Reconstruction instead scores the valid waveform prefix when duration is recorded. The two paths therefore have distinct padding conventions.

\subsection{Probe and metric definitions}

Linear probes standardize features using each training fold and fit logistic regression with $C=1$ and a 3,000-iteration limit; both age and gender use balanced class weights. Speaker ID uses mean pooling; generic emotion, age, and gender use mean+standard-deviation pooling. Reported classification scores are arithmetic means of per-fold scores. Macro-F1 uses labels present in the fold's gold labels or predictions, as in the evaluator's default scoring; balanced accuracy averages recall over gold labels present in that fold. Rare-class absence therefore changes the set of scored labels across folds.

The majority-label reference is computed from saved out-of-fold gold labels without feature extraction or model fitting. For each held-out fold, the most frequent label in the other folds is predicted for every test item, and scores are averaged over folds using the same conventions. The majority is \texttt{twenties} for age and \texttt{male\_masculine} for gender in every fold. Full/reduced age balanced accuracy is 0.240/0.300 and macro-F1 is 0.216701/0.274245; gender balanced accuracy is 0.500 in both and macro-F1 is 0.437859/0.443730. The same fold-wise convention is used for CLAE and the reference.

Reconstruction runs in FP32 and scores each valid source prefix before averaging over clips. MR-STFT uses FFT sizes $[256,512,1024,2048]$, hop length one quarter of each FFT size, full-length Hann windows, centered analysis, and log-magnitude epsilon $10^{-3}$. Spectral-convergence, magnitude, and log-magnitude weights are $0.1$, $1$, and $1$, respectively. STOI and ESTOI use \texttt{pystoi}; wide-band PESQ uses \texttt{pesq} at 16 kHz. Each requested reconstruction metric was computed successfully for all 400 full-data or 100 reduced-subset items. The SI-SDR limitation remains as discussed in the main text.

Verification uses cosine similarity on mean or mean+standard-deviation embeddings; the tables select mean+standard-deviation pooling. Full evaluation scores all unique pairs of 2,000 utterances from 334 speakers. Reduced evaluation has 800 utterances from 134 speakers, with 1,996 target and 18,004 nontarget trials. The capped sampler preferentially retains target pairs before sampling nontarget pairs; it is not uniform all-pairs subsampling. EER averages false-positive and false-negative rates at the nearest ROC crossing. Normalized minDCF uses target prior 0.01 and unit miss/false-alarm costs.

The attention-ASR implementation uses a two-layer, four-head Transformer decoder ($d=256$, feed-forward dimension 1,024), batch size 16, and an 8,000-update budget. Candidate audio is duration-validated at 15 seconds, encoded in three-second windows, and concatenated without utterance pooling. The full-data ASR development set contains 10,000 items. CER and WER are error ratios, so WER may exceed one. These are fixed-budget development-set diagnostics, not a held-out ASR benchmark.

Temporal probes use a 300-frame cap; the attentive-statistics head has 128 hidden units and batch size 64. The full-data protocol specifies 40 attention epochs and 30 Transformer epochs; the reduced protocol specifies 20 epochs for both. Historical optimizer and stopping settings are incompletely recorded, which limits exact replication. Full neural-probe aggregates use probe seeds 0/1/2 with fixed data/split seeds 0; main and reduced comparisons use probe seed 0. Deterministic linear-probe repeats have identical scalar scores and are not treated as independent training replications.

External feature extractors remain frozen: WavLM-base-plus and Whisper-tiny use final hidden states; WavLM is final-layer only, not a learned weighted layer aggregation. ECAPA uses its utterance embedding; emotion2vec uses continuous FunASR frame features; Mimi uses its continuous pre-quantization latent; and Higgs Audio V2 decodes its quantizer output to continuous vectors. No codec probe substitutes integer code IDs for continuous features. The separate Mimi reconstruction comparator fixes eight quantizers (nominal 1.1~kbps), rather than reusing the pre-quantization probe feature.

\subsection{Training configuration and reproducibility}

The matched ablations use 25,000 training steps with a 100,000-step scheduler horizon. The training configuration specifies seed 42, AdamW with $\beta=(0.9,0.999)$, epsilon $10^{-8}$, weight decay $10^{-5}$, and 5,000 warmup steps. Full-objective loss weights are $1/0.3/0.7$ for reconstruction/view/VISReg; reconstruction-only sets the latter two to zero, and representation-only sets the first to zero. No-mHC disables mHC; 25 Hz changes the frontend/decoder stride product to 640; no-decoder-corruption disables its mask and noise.

The archived experiments do not provide a complete historical software environment, hardware record, or resolved configuration for the main pretraining run. Exact weight identity across the full-data and reduced evaluations has not been independently verified. These gaps limit exact end-to-end replication; the stated training budgets and evaluation protocols should not be read as a complete execution record.
