\begin{abstract}
We study a compact representation encoder for 16~kHz Bengali speech. Its 15.29M-parameter frontend and encoder produce 256-dimensional features $z$ at 12.5 frames/s for downstream frozen-feature discrimination tasks; the full training model has 23.80M parameters. The 12.5~Hz resolution aligns with low-frame-rate neural audio codecs and tokenizers such as Mimi, DualCodec, and NanoCodec \citep{defossez2024moshi,li2025dualcodec,casanova2025nanocodec}, while CLAE remains continuous: its floating-point latent is neither quantized nor entropy-coded, so its scalar rate is not bitrate-comparable. A separate per-frame projector maps $z$ to a 64-dimensional space $p$. Training uses waveform reconstruction as an auxiliary self-supervised pretext and information-retention objective, non-contrastive global/local view consistency in $p$ to encourage nuisance-invariant features, and Variance-Invariance-Sketching Regularization (VISReg) in $p$ to support non-collapsed, well-distributed representations. At the selected 210k-step checkpoint, frozen $z$ features support the reported emotion, age, gender, and speaker probes; reconstruction on 400 Common Voice Bengali validation-manifest clips gives MR-STFT 0.747, STOI 0.850, ESTOI 0.762, and PESQ-WB 2.146. A matched 25k-step study shows that removing reconstruction worsens reconstruction metrics and two temporal-emotion probes in this setting. The main comparisons use one seed and objective reconstruction metrics; broader evaluation is needed to establish generality.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

Compact speech encoders are useful when their frozen features retain information for several downstream discrimination tasks without requiring task-specific encoder adaptation. This frozen-feature framing follows speech representation work such as wav2vec~2.0 and HuBERT \citep{baevski2020wav2vec2,hsu2021hubert} and the lightweight-head evaluation protocol of SUPERB \citep{yang2021superb}. Modern neural audio codecs and tokenizers also commonly operate at low temporal resolutions, making 12.5~Hz a practical design point for a compact speech representation; DualCodec and NanoCodec, for example, report 12.5~Hz codec configurations \citep{li2025dualcodec,casanova2025nanocodec}. This does not make the systems bitrate- or architecture-equivalent. CLAE is continuous rather than a codec: its floating-point latent is neither quantized nor entropy-coded, so its scalar rate is not directly comparable to codec bitrate.

We present CLAE, a 23.80M-parameter continuous-latent training model trained on an approximately 2,000-hour inventory assembled from four Bengali speech sources. Its 15.29M-parameter frontend and encoder produce a 256-dimensional frame-level representation $z$ at 12.5~Hz; downstream probes keep this feature extractor frozen and consume $z$. A distinct 64-dimensional projector output $p$ is used only by two representation objectives: direct global/local view consistency and Variance-Invariance-Sketching Regularization (VISReg; \citealp{wu2026visreg}). View consistency encourages features stable to the augmentation and masking nuisances used to construct the views. VISReg supports a centered, scaled, and distributed projector population, countering degenerate representations. Reconstruction is an auxiliary self-supervised pretext and information-retention objective: the decoder generates a waveform from a corrupted copy of $z$, and the generated waveform is compared with the clean source in an 80-bin log-mel domain.

The contribution is empirical and implementation-specific. We provide: (i) a compact continuous 12.5~Hz Bengali speech representation encoder evaluated through frozen-feature discrimination probes; (ii) a training objective that combines reconstruction for self-supervised information retention, non-contrastive view consistency for nuisance invariance, and frame-level VISReg for anti-collapse and distribution regularization; (iii) a Conformer-derived encoder with a project adaptation of Manifold-Constrained Hyper-Connections (mHC); and (iv) evaluations of the selected 210k-step checkpoint together with a matched 25k-step ablation suite. We assess the combined design through frozen-feature probes and ablations; the individual architectural and objective components build on prior work.

\section{Related Work}

\paragraph{Joint-embedding learning.}
Image JEPA introduced representation-space prediction with distinct context and target pathways \citep{assran2023ijepa}; A-JEPA adapted masked-target JEPA ideas to audio \citep{fei2023ajepa}. LeJEPA instead studies direct view agreement with isotropic-Gaussian regularization \citep{balestriero2025lejepa}. CLAE uses a JEPA-inspired, non-contrastive per-frame view-agreement loss, but it is not canonical predictive JEPA: it has one shared encoder and no target encoder, exponential moving average, learned predictor, stop-gradient target path, masked-region prediction, or negative pairs. It also differs from LeJEPA in domain, waveform view construction, grouped consistency loss, frame-level population, supporting regularizer, and reconstruction.

\paragraph{Distribution regularization.}
VICReg combines invariance, per-dimension variance control, and covariance regularization \citep{bardes2022vicreg}. VISReg retains explicit center and scale terms but replaces covariance control with random one-dimensional projections whose sorted samples are matched to Gaussian quantiles \citep{wu2026visreg}. We use VISReg to regularize the distribution of projector features and discourage collapse. Our frame-level adaptation is described in Section~\ref{sec:visreg}.

\paragraph{Continuous and discrete audio representations.}
Continuous Audio Language Models use a continuous variational bottleneck, KL regularization, additional speech distillation, and generative next-frame modeling \citep{rouard2025calm}; those components are not used here. UniWav studies joint representation and generation objectives for speech \citep{liu2025uniwav}. By contrast, UniAudio and UniTok-Audio illustrate autoregressive generation over discrete audio tokens \citep{yang2024uniaudio,liu2025unitokaudio}. Neural codecs such as SoundStream and EnCodec learn quantized, bitrate-controlled representations \citep{zeghidour2021soundstream,defossez2023encodec}. Mimi, introduced with Moshi, is a separately pretrained common-evaluation reconstruction reference in our 25k study \citep{defossez2024moshi}. CLAE's continuous floating-point latent is neither quantized nor entropy-coded, so its scalar rate is not directly comparable to codec bitrate.

\paragraph{Sequence encoder and residual connections.}
The encoder builds on the Conformer macaron block \citep{gulati2020conformer}. It incorporates squeeze-and-excitation but does not adopt the full downsampling and local-attention design of FastConformer \citep{rekesh2023fastconformer}. Selected layers use a project adaptation of Manifold-Constrained Hyper-Connections \citep{xie2025mhc}.

\section{Method}

\subsection{Architecture}

Figure~\ref{fig:architecture} summarizes the architecture. For a 16~kHz mono waveform $x$, the convolutional frontend has five Conv1D stages with channels $[128,256,384,512,512]$, kernels $[10,8,8,4,4]$, and strides $[5,4,4,4,4]$, with GroupNorm and GELU. Its total stride is 1,280 samples, yielding approximately 12.5 frames/s. An eight-layer, 256-dimensional Conformer-derived encoder uses eight attention heads, a 1,024-dimensional feed-forward sublayer, rotary full self-attention, macaron feed-forward modules, a 9-tap convolution branch, dropout 0.1, and squeeze-and-excitation. It produces
\begin{equation}
z = E(F(x)), \qquad z\in\mathbb{R}^{T'\times256}.
\end{equation}
A per-frame MLP projector with dimensions $256\rightarrow512\rightarrow64$ produces $p=P(z)$. The decoder and downstream frozen-feature probes use $z$, whereas the consistency and VISReg objectives use $p$.

The FiLM-conditioned waveform decoder starts with 768 channels, reverses the frontend with strides $[4,4,4,4,5]$, and uses two residual blocks per upsampling stage with dilations $[1,3,9]$. The full training model has 23,803,125 parameters. The frontend, encoder, projector, and decoder contain 2,890,240, 12,403,220, 165,440, and 8,344,225 parameters, respectively. The frozen frontend--encoder feature extractor therefore contains 15,293,460 parameters.

\subsection{Waveform views and consistency objective}

Training first samples one three-second source segment, and every view is derived from that same pre-cropped waveform. Two global views receive independently sampled waveform augmentation. Four local views remain full-length three-second waveforms but receive stronger waveform augmentation and waveform-aligned span masking; their frontend features can receive an additional independent frame mask. The waveform augmentations are gain, additive noise, centered low-pass filtering, and clipping only: they do not shift, speed-perturb, resample, independently crop, or temporally warp the signal. Waveform masks are constructed on the 1,280-sample frontend-frame grid and expanded onto the corresponding waveform samples; the optional frontend mask is also defined on that frame grid. Thus, frame index $t$ refers to the same time interval across views, although its waveform content may be augmented or masked. All six views pass through the same frontend, encoder, and projector.

Let $p_g$ and $p_l$ denote projector features grouped into global and local views. For each example and frame, the center $\bar p_g$ is the mean of the two global projector features. The implemented consistency loss is
\begin{equation}
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{view}}=
\operatorname{mean}\!\left[(p_g-\bar p_g)^2\right]
+\operatorname{mean}\!\left[(p_l-\bar p_g)^2\right].
\label{eq:view}
\end{equation}
The two group means are added, so global and local groups have equal aggregate contribution despite containing two and four views. This JEPA-inspired objective is non-contrastive per-frame global/local agreement, not canonical context-to-target or masked-region prediction, and it is not a direct utterance-identity or class-discrimination loss. It encourages invariance to the specified augmentation and masking nuisances, which can be useful for the downstream discrimination probes, but those probes are separate evaluations of $z$.

\subsection{VISReg adaptation}
\label{sec:visreg}

Figure~\ref{fig:visreg} outlines the regularizer. The supporting VISReg implementation flattens projector frames from all views and examples into one population; it does not pool them. Under distributed training, the population is gathered across workers. Its effective shape is $N=1$, $B=T'VB_{\mathrm{global}}$, and $D=64$. For population points $q_i\in\mathbb{R}^D$, it penalizes the population mean, centers the points, computes a per-dimension RMS scale with division by $\sqrt{B}$, and penalizes deviation from unit scale. Normalization uses a detached scale. On every forward pass, 256 normalized Gaussian directions are sampled. Values projected onto each direction are sorted and matched to standard-normal quantiles $\Phi^{-1}(i/(B+1))$. Center, scale, and projected-distribution shape terms have equal internal weight.

\begin{figure*}[t]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{figures/tikz/visreg_adaptation.pdf}
\caption{Supporting project-specific VISReg constraint. Projector frames from every view and example are gathered into one distributed population, centered and scaled, projected onto 256 freshly sampled normalized directions, sorted, and matched to standard-normal quantiles.}
\label{fig:visreg}
\end{figure*}

This implements the core VISReg center/scale/shape construction with project-specific frame-level and distributed adaptations. Our adaptation gathers frames across workers, adds $10^{-6}$ to the scale for numerical stability, assigns equal weights to the three terms, and casts target quantiles to the activation dtype. Training uses BF16 precision.

\subsection{Reconstruction and total objective}

The decoder receives $z$ from the first global view. Before decoding, an independent span mask removes 50\% of frames in spans of 4--24 frames and Gaussian noise with standard deviation 0.03 is added. The target remains the clean source waveform. The decoder outputs a waveform, which is compared with the clean waveform using an 80-bin log-mel loss with FFT size 1,024, window length 1,024, hop length 256, a Hann window, magnitude weight 1.0, log-magnitude weight 1.0, and spectral-convergence weight 0. Multi-resolution STFT is used only for evaluation.

The selected objective is
\begin{equation}
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{total}}=
1.0\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{mel}}+
0.3\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{view}}+
0.7\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{VISReg}}.
\end{equation}
The three coefficients weight losses with different numerical scales. Training uses no quantization, variational bottleneck, or adversarial objective.

\subsection{mHC adaptation}

At zero-indexed encoder layers 2 and 5, mHC widens the residual path to two streams. It applies learned static pre/post mixing and a Sinkhorn-constrained residual matrix with identity blending, uses ten Sinkhorn iterations, wraps complete encoder layers, and returns to one stream by a project-specific uniform mean readout. These choices adapt the reference mHC design rather than reproduce its complete large-model implementation. Section~\ref{sec:ablation} reports mixed, probe-dependent evidence for this component.

\section{Data and Training}

The training manifests combine four Bengali speech sources, with 1,310,076 utterances and close to 2,000 total hours of audio: Common Voice \citep{ardila2020commonvoice}, OpenSLR-53 \citep{kjartansson2018crowdsourced}, RegSpeech12 \citep{hassan2025regspeech12}, and Shrutilipi \citep{bhogale2023shrutilipi}. Table~\ref{tab:training-data} gives the source inventory. The manifests use a 95/5 stratified train/validation split with seed 42; Table~\ref{tab:training-split} gives the resulting counts. No exact audited unique duration is available. Common Voice and OpenSLR-53 both contribute to pretraining, so evaluations on those sources are in-domain frozen-feature evaluations with unassessed train--evaluation overlap. The inventory does not establish demographic or dialect balance, and no deduplication or train--evaluation leakage audit was performed. SUBESCO \citep{sultana2021subesco}, used for emotion evaluation, is absent from the documented training inventory.

We report the terminal checkpoint after 210,000 training steps, without downstream-metric checkpoint selection. Training uses BF16, AdamW, batch size 42, gradient accumulation over four batches, and gradient clipping at 1.0. The learning-rate schedule was adjusted during training: the base learning rate was reduced to $5\times10^{-4}$ at step 50,000, and later continuations used $10^{-4}$ with a 300,000-step scheduler horizon. Training ended at step 210,000. Thus, the trajectory includes manual schedule changes rather than a single uninterrupted cosine schedule.

With batch size 42 and four accumulation batches on one worker, the nominal effective batch is 168 source segments. If this batch construction is maintained across the trajectory, 210,000 training steps correspond to 35.28M sampled three-second segments, or 29,400 hours of exposure including repeated sampling. These are exposure estimates, not audited counts of unique audio or successful parameter updates. Each source segment produces six augmented views.

There is no active in-loop validation or validation-based early stopping. The terminal checkpoint is reported without a downstream-metric sweep.

\section{Evaluation}
\label{sec:evaluation}

We evaluate reconstruction quality and frozen-feature performance on downstream speech tasks. The selected 210k checkpoint is evaluated separately from the matched 25k ablations; their absolute scores should not be interpreted as a checkpoint-matched comparison. Reconstruction metrics are objective proxies. PESQ \citep{rix2001pesq}, STOI \citep{taal2010stoi}, ESTOI \citep{jensen2016estoi}, and MR-STFT \citep{yamamoto2020parallelwavegan} do not substitute for listener judgments.

Appendix~\ref{sec:replication} specifies feature extraction, metric definitions, label inventories, and replication constraints. Where available, we compare frozen CLAE features with WavLM \citep{chen2022wavlm}, Whisper \citep{radford2023whisper}, ECAPA-TDNN \citep{desplanques2020ecapa}, emotion2vec \citep{ma2024emotion2vec}, Mimi \citep{defossez2024moshi}, and Higgs Audio V2 tokenizer \citep{boson2025higgsaudiov2tokenizer}. These are purpose-built systems with different training objectives and specializations; they are reported as task-specific references rather than a homogeneous family of representation models.

CLAE at 210k steps has the lowest STFT loss, while the 25k no-decoder-corruption condition leads PESQ, STOI, age, speaker identification, and both temporal-emotion probes among CLAE conditions. In the direct reduced comparison, reconstruction-only leads generic emotion descriptively (43.89 vs.~41.59 macro-F1), whereas the full-objective model is slightly higher on age (26.11 vs.~25.89) and the two temporal probes (35.21 vs.~34.97 and 42.30 vs.~41.13). These one-seed differences are small and mixed, so they do not demonstrate a uniform benefit of the view-consistency-plus-VISReg branch. The 25~Hz condition leads gender and verification. All CLAE age macro-F1 values in this reduced study are below the majority-label reference (27.42\%); this limits any claim of demographic utility. Representation-only is a negative reconstruction control and also trails the other trained CLAE conditions on every representation task in this reduced protocol.

Whisper-tiny leads generic emotion, Mimi leads age, and ECAPA leads gender, closed-set speaker identification, and verification. WavLM has the strongest configured temporal-Transformer baseline. The eight-quantizer Mimi reconstruction reference obtains STFT 0.8949 on the same 100 clips. Table~\ref{tab:reduced-fp32-baselines} reports the external-reference scores for this protocol.

\section{Limitations}

Training did not use validation-based model selection. The main 210k probe results and the matched 25k temporal ablations use one probe seed. We report no paired-bootstrap analysis, confidence intervals, statistical significance, stability, or robustness claims. No human listening study was conducted, so reconstruction evidence is limited to objective proxy metrics. The saved SI-SDR measurements are unexpectedly low (about $-23$ to $-25$ dB for reconstruction-capable CLAE conditions in the reduced study, versus $+2.05$ dB for Mimi). Their disagreement with the spectral and intelligibility proxies remains unresolved; we withhold SI-SDR from the comparison tables pending a waveform-alignment and phase-fidelity investigation. The remaining metrics do not establish sample-level waveform fidelity. We did not compare alternative distribution regularizers under a controlled protocol.

The training inventory was not audited for demographic or dialect coverage, deduplication, or leakage. Common Voice and OpenSLR evaluations are in-domain because both sources contribute to pretraining, and their train--evaluation overlap is unknown. Demographic probes predict dataset-supplied labels rather than independently verified age or gender. The exact corpus releases and training--evaluation overlap remain insufficiently documented, limiting interpretation beyond these samples. The all-pairs speaker-verification protocol is not a standard enrollment/test benchmark. We draw no conclusion about linguistic retention. The 25~Hz ablation changes frame rate, and mHC effects are mixed. VISReg and mHC are recent preprints used through project-specific adaptations; the reported findings concern the exact local implementation and configuration.

\section{Ethical Considerations}

Speech can identify speakers and reveal sensitive attributes. The age and gender probes measure predictability of corpus labels; they do not validate those labels as a complete account of identity or justify individual-level decisions. Speaker representations can enable unauthorized identification, surveillance, or impersonation. These research evaluations do not establish suitability for deployment in such settings. Bengali speech and acted-emotion results also should not be generalized to unrepresented dialects, populations, or spontaneous emotion.

The study uses third-party speech corpora. Their availability does not by itself establish consent for demographic inference or speaker identification. The corpus-specific consent terms, release versions, and redistribution permissions require verification before any audio redistribution; this paper makes no claim that those permissions have been independently audited. No new human listening study or participant recruitment was conducted.

\paragraph{Availability.}
We plan to release the source code, evaluation recipes, and model checkpoints through a public repository. Dataset manifests will not be released. Third-party audio must be obtained from its providers under the applicable terms. The absence of released manifests will limit exact reconstruction of the sampled subsets; the paper therefore records sample counts, label inventories, and split fingerprints where available.

\section{Conclusion}

CLAE is a compact 12.5~Hz continuous Bengali speech representation encoder trained with reconstruction as an auxiliary self-supervised information-retention objective, non-contrastive global/local view consistency for nuisance invariance, and frame-level VISReg for anti-collapse and distribution regularization. Its frozen features support emotion and speaker discrimination under the evaluated protocols, while the age probe does not outperform the majority-label reference in macro-F1. The matched 25k study shows a setting-specific benefit from retaining reconstruction, a frame-rate trade-off at 25~Hz, mixed mHC effects, and a metric-dependent comparison with Mimi. The reconstruction-only comparison gives mixed evidence for adding the view-consistency and VISReg branch. CLAE remains continuous and is not bitrate-comparable to quantized codecs. Broader task coverage and repeated-training experiments are needed to assess generality.

\section{Replication Details}
\label{sec:replication}

CLAE probes use frozen final encoder features $z$, without layer aggregation or projector features. Audio is mixed to mono and resampled to 16 kHz. CLAE encodes non-overlapping three-second windows, right-pads the final window, and concatenates their frames. Generic utterance probes pool all returned frames, including final-window padding. Reconstruction instead scores the valid waveform prefix when duration is recorded. The two paths therefore have distinct padding conventions.

\subsection{Probe and metric definitions}

Linear probes standardize features using each training fold and fit logistic regression with $C=1$ and a 3,000-iteration limit; both age and gender use balanced class weights. Speaker ID uses mean pooling; generic emotion, age, and gender use mean+standard-deviation pooling. Reported classification scores are arithmetic means of per-fold scores. Macro-F1 uses labels present in the fold's gold labels or predictions, as in the evaluator's default scoring; balanced accuracy averages recall over gold labels present in that fold. Rare-class absence therefore changes the set of scored labels across folds.

The majority-label reference is computed from saved out-of-fold gold labels without feature extraction or model fitting. For each held-out fold, the most frequent label in the other folds is predicted for every test item, and scores are averaged over folds using the same conventions. The majority is \texttt{twenties} for age and \texttt{male\_masculine} for gender in every fold. Full/reduced age balanced accuracy is 0.240/0.300 and macro-F1 is 0.216701/0.274245; gender balanced accuracy is 0.500 in both and macro-F1 is 0.437859/0.443730. Recomputing the saved CLAE predictions under this scoring convention reproduces their reported macro-F1.

Reconstruction runs in FP32 and scores each valid source prefix before averaging over clips. MR-STFT uses FFT sizes $[256,512,1024,2048]$, hop length one quarter of each FFT size, full-length Hann windows, centered analysis, and log-magnitude epsilon $10^{-3}$. Spectral-convergence, magnitude, and log-magnitude weights are $0.1$, $1$, and $1$, respectively. STOI and ESTOI use \texttt{pystoi}; wide-band PESQ uses \texttt{pesq} at 16 kHz. The selected reconstruction outputs record successful coverage for every requested metric on all 400 or 100 items. Coverage establishes computation, not perceptual validity; the unresolved SI-SDR issue is discussed in the main text.

Verification uses cosine similarity on mean or mean+standard-deviation embeddings; the tables select mean+standard-deviation pooling. Full evaluation scores all unique pairs of 2,000 utterances from 334 speakers. Reduced evaluation has 800 utterances from 134 speakers, with 1,996 target and 18,004 nontarget trials. The capped sampler preferentially retains target pairs before sampling nontarget pairs; it is not uniform all-pairs subsampling. EER averages false-positive and false-negative rates at the nearest ROC crossing. Normalized minDCF uses target prior 0.01 and unit miss/false-alarm costs.

The attention-ASR implementation uses a two-layer, four-head Transformer decoder ($d=256$, feed-forward dimension 1,024), batch size 16, and an 8,000-update budget. Candidate audio is duration-validated at 15 seconds, encoded in three-second windows, and concatenated without utterance pooling. The full-data development prediction artifact contains 10,000 items. CER and WER are error ratios, so WER may exceed one. These are fixed-budget development-set diagnostics, not a held-out ASR benchmark.

Temporal recipes use a 300-frame cap; the attentive-statistics head has 128 hidden units and batch size 64. The full-suite recipe specifies 40 attention epochs and 30 Transformer epochs; the reduced study report specifies 20 epochs for both. Not all historical optimizer and stopping settings survive in scalar-result metadata, so implementation defaults alone do not establish an exact historical rerun. Full neural-probe aggregates use probe seeds 0/1/2 with fixed data/split seeds 0; main and reduced comparisons use probe seed 0. Deterministic linear-probe repeats have identical scalar scores and are not treated as independent training replications.


External feature extractors remain frozen: WavLM-base-plus and Whisper-tiny use final hidden states; WavLM is final-layer only, not a learned weighted layer aggregation. ECAPA uses its utterance embedding; emotion2vec uses continuous FunASR frame features; Mimi uses its continuous pre-quantization latent; and Higgs Audio V2 decodes its quantizer output to continuous vectors. No codec probe substitutes integer code IDs for continuous features. The separate Mimi reconstruction comparator fixes eight quantizers (nominal 1.1~kbps), rather than reusing the pre-quantization probe feature.
